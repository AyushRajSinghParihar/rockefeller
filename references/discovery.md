# Discover the business constraint

Use this walkthrough when the user requests business office hours, demand is unproven, or the proposed investment may solve the wrong problem. Use supplied evidence first. A clear, fully specified operating decision does not need a discovery interview.

## Follow the evidence

In a conversational session, ask the most consequential unresolved question, use the answer, and follow its implications before moving on. A short group of related questions is useful when the user wants a written intake. Skip resolved questions. If the user wants a provisional answer now, give one with the assumption that could change it.

Adapt the investigation to the stage:

- **Before launch:** identify the buyer, a recent occurrence of the problem, the current workaround, and the time, money, or consequences involved. Establish how to reach that buyer and what would cause a purchase.
- **With users:** examine observed workflows, activation, repeated use, abandonment, and the gap between the user's description of value and the founder's pitch.
- **With paying customers:** examine renewal, repeat purchase, expansion, service burden, contribution, and whether the newest customers behave like earlier cohorts.

Treat evidence according to what it establishes. Interview enthusiasm and waitlists show interest; observed effort shows a problem worth investigating; paid trials show a willingness to try at particular terms; repeat purchases and retained contribution support a stronger operating claim. None alone establishes profitable scale. A payment may reflect a large discount, and a letter of intent may have no binding purchase obligation. Record the source, date, segment, terms, and limitations. Lack of an existing workaround is a question to investigate, not proof that no demand exists.

When product or retention spending is proposed, investigate a real customer journey: what the customer attempted, where they struggled, what surprised the operator, and whether anyone observed it without guiding the user. A requested feature is evidence of a possible need, not an automatic build instruction. Do not invent customer quotations or claim an interview occurred.

## Challenge the premise proportionately

For a material commitment, identify the strongest assumption connecting the proposed action to the desired outcome. Compare it with the best supplied contrary evidence. What happens without the commitment, and could a smaller intervention address the actual constraint?

For example, if completed goods wait for release while production has spare capacity, investigate release delays before recommending a larger factory. If contracted demand genuinely exceeds capacity and incremental returns and liquidity support expansion, recommend the investment. Premise checking should improve the choice, not become a reflexive refusal.

## Make the next experiment concrete

Describe one customer segment, one costly problem, a feasible deliverable, a price hypothesis, and a way to reach buyers. Compare a manual service or narrow pilot with building a full platform when it tests the same uncertainty. Match timing to the industry and buying process rather than requiring a sale within an arbitrary week.

Specify what result would establish willingness to pay and what separate evidence would establish profitable delivery. Bound the cash and effort commitment using known resources; identify what remains unknown. End with the immediate evidence-gathering action and a scale, revise, or stop condition. A proposed price or acceptance target is a test assumption until a buyer accepts it.
