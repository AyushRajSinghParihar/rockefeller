# Pricing decisions

Use when price or packaging could change the operating priority. A simple margin question needs only the calculation.

Separate three choices: **packaging** (what is included), **pricing metric** (what the customer pays per), and **price** (the amount). Establish the relevant segment, buyer, customer value, current alternative, and costs of switching. A competitor's price is evidence about an alternative, not a prescription for this business's economics. Survey price acceptance is not an observed demand curve.

## Compare contribution at plausible volumes

Use net prices after applicable discounts, refunds, and pass-through taxes. Identify all incremental costs, including fulfillment, usage, payment fees, support, returns, and relevant acquisition spending. Keep their treatment consistent with the ledger and avoid double counting. Segment high-support or high-consumption customers when averages hide losses.

For unchanged fixed costs and a comparable product mix, contribution is:

`units × (net price − variable cost per unit)`

To preserve current total contribution when the new unit contribution is positive:

`required new units = current units × current unit contribution / new unit contribution`

This is a decision threshold, not a volume forecast or a cohort-retention estimate. Round up when units must be whole. State the assumptions; incorporate changed costs, mix, acquisition, or fixed-cost steps when relevant. With nonpositive new unit contribution, this volume formula cannot establish an attractive scale decision.

Example: 1,000 units at $100 with $60 variable cost produce $40,000 contribution. At $110 with the same cost, unit contribution becomes $50, requiring 800 units, or 80% of current volume. At 850 units contribution is $42,500; at 750 it is $37,500. A 10% price increase does not imply that only a 10% volume decline is affordable. If service costs change with the package, recompute the threshold.

For usage or bundled pricing, test heavy-use customers and support obligations as well as the average. Annual prepayment changes cash timing and creates delivery/refund obligations; collections are not automatically earned profit. Consider cannibalization and migration between packages when comparing total contribution.

## Learn before broad rollout

Compare a few justified price/package hypotheses and identify which uncertain response changes the choice. When demand response is unknown, propose a bounded, interpretable test with a baseline, relevant customer segment, cash/effort limit, and review or rollback trigger. Track contribution, conversion, repeat purchase or retention, refunds, service burden, and cash timing as applicable. Separate a new-customer test from changes affecting existing contracts. Do not prescribe a universal sample size, rollout percentage, discount, churn tolerance, or notice period.

If data is too sparse for a reliable causal estimate, say what the observations support and what they do not. Treat the test price and candidate volumes as hypotheses, and respect existing contractual commitments.
